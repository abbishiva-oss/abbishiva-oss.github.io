Extracting claims and evidence from research papers

Abbilash Shivaparan, Ayomikun Ogunmolasuyi · Advised by Prof. Joel Chan · University of Maryland, Fall 2026
Summary

Living reviews only work if breaking new papers into claims and evidence is cheap. We're testing whether AI can do that well enough to trust, by comparing a frontier model, a local model, and human review on the same set of papers. Early review suggests a lot of what the AI writes still needs a person to fix it, and some of those fixes change what a result actually means.

The problem

Synthesis is when you pull together everything a field knows about one question, and it takes forever. One review we looked at screened almost 3,700 references just to end up with 28 studies. On top of that, its search stopped in 2005, so nothing newer is in it. "Living" reviews fix that by updating every time a new study comes out, but that only works if updating is cheap.

The expensive part is breaking each new paper down into its claims and the evidence behind them. Joel's lab stores research that way in something called a discourse graph, where every claim and every piece of evidence is its own node and they all link to each other. If AI could do that breakdown, living reviews would actually be doable. So our question is pretty simple: is AI good enough at it that we can trust it?

Here's what that actually looks like. This is one small corner of the lab's graph, pulled straight from the vault: one research question, the claim that answers it, the evidence behind that claim, and two patterns that show up across papers.

Background

The research we built on comes from a few directions, and it helped to understand each piece before we got into the data.

  • Reviews go stale. The interpreter review we keep coming back to (Karliner et al., 2007) is a good example of how much work one synthesis takes and how fast it ages. Elliott et al. (2014) argue this is a problem with systematic reviews in general and propose living reviews that update as new studies come out.
  • Standard RAG has limits. Retrieval-augmented generation pulls the text chunks most similar to a question and hands them to a language model (Lewis et al., 2020). It works well when the answer is in one passage, but synthesis questions spread the answer across a whole collection.
  • Structure helps. Edge et al. (2024) showed that organizing documents into a graph first beat standard RAG on questions about a whole corpus.
  • Store claims, not documents. Chan et al. (2024) argue the real problem is that research gets stored as whole papers, when synthesis actually needs claims, the evidence behind them, and how they relate. That's the idea behind discourse graphs, and it's the foundation of Joel's lab.

Put together, the open question is whether the step that turns papers into a discourse graph can be handed to AI. If it can't be trusted, the whole pipeline breaks at step one.

Data and method

27papers on using AI to evaluate research
205nodes already drafted by a frontier model
0of those reviewed by a person when we started
3sources compared: frontier model, local model, human review

The dataset is the lab's Obsidian vault. Every claim, piece of evidence, research question, caveat, and cross-paper pattern is its own note, and the notes link to each other, so you can actually click through the graph. A frontier model, Claude, had already gone through all 27 papers and drafted the nodes.

Each node gets reviewed and labeled Correct, Edit, or Delete, with a short note explaining why. That human review becomes the reference. Then we run a local open model, like Gemma or Llama, on the same papers and score both models against it:

  • Precision: out of everything the model pulled out, how much was actually right.
  • Recall: out of everything that should have been pulled out, how much the model found.
  • F1: one number that balances the two.
  • Error types: what kind of mistake it was, because a missing detail and a changed meaning aren't the same problem.

Before we split the work, Ayomikun and I both review the same set of nodes on our own and compare our labels with each other and with the lab's reviewer. If we don't agree with each other, our labels aren't worth much.

Early findings

So far, most of the nodes that have been reviewed needed at least some editing. Some research questions even had to be split into two. To be fair, "Edit" doesn't mean the AI was totally wrong, and a lot of the fixes are small. When we grouped the reviewer's notes by what was actually wrong, they fell into two kinds of problems.

The most common issue was missing context. The ones that worry me more are the reframes, where the way the AI wrote a node changed what the result actually meant. In one case, fixing it flipped a result from looking bad to looking really good. That's exactly the kind of mistake you can't let slide into a review people rely on.

How the plan changed

Our original question was different. We were going to test whether a discourse graph finds better evidence than keyword search (BM25) or standard RAG, using the same papers and about 20 synthesis questions about language access in healthcare, scored with precision, recall, and nDCG.

Honestly, our project looks pretty different now. Once we got into the real data, we found out half the study basically already existed. The AI's output was sitting right there, and what was missing was the human side to compare it against.

This version is more realistic for one semester. It's also closer to how a living review would really work, where a person checks what the AI drafts instead of doing everything by hand.

Challenges

Not everything went smoothly, and some of the blockers were things I never would've guessed going in.

The repo wouldn't open on Windows

Some notes are named after research questions, so their file names have a question mark in them. Windows doesn't allow that, so git couldn't check out the files at all.

What we didUsed a Windows-friendly zip from the lab, and asked about renaming those files so git works normally.
We couldn't push edits back

The zip was read-only, so we couldn't update the shared vault directly.

What we didLogged our labels in the lab's benchmarking sheet and on an experiment page Joel set up for us in Roam.
The plan didn't match the data

We planned to annotate from scratch, but the AI's nodes already existed.

What we didSwitched to reviewing the existing nodes the way the lab does, and confirmed the change with Joel.
Minor vs. major is hard to call

After one practice node, it's hard to tell a small fix from a big one.

What we didUsed the lab reviewer's notes as a guide, and both of us review the same nodes first to check we agree.

My role

I'm leading the local model and the scoring. That means setting up an open model, running it on the same 27 papers, and building the scoring that compares it and the frontier model against the human reviews. Ayomikun leads the node review and keeps our labeling guide consistent. We also sit down with the lab every Friday to annotate together, which is where most of my questions get answered.

What I've learned so far

  • The data decides the plan. We spent weeks on a plan that changed the moment we opened the real dataset. Now I'd look at the data before getting attached to a method.
  • "Correct" is harder to define than it sounds. Two people can read the same node and disagree on whether it needs an edit. That's why checking agreement first matters so much.
  • A strong model still needs a person. Even a frontier model's output wasn't ready to go straight into a review. The interesting question isn't whether AI makes mistakes, it's which ones, and how much they matter.

Other research

Reaching new young voters in New Carrollton. A research pitch for a city council. New Carrollton has talked about letting 16 and 17 year olds vote in local elections, and if that happens, the city suddenly has a group of voters who probably don't follow city channels at all. Our pitch was simple: don't assume teens look for information the same way adults do. Find out first.

We designed an online survey about where teens would actually look for local election info, what confuses them, and which formats would help, plus one open question so they could tell us what we missed. We also used 2010 to 2020 Census data to show the city is growing and getting more diverse, which is why we recommended reaching young voters through schools, community networks, and more than one language instead of just the city website.

DataProcessBot

Desktop app for asking questions about your own documents2026
Source code ↗
Why I built it

During my Pfizer externship I built a RAG pipeline that could answer questions about pharma documents. It worked, but it lived in a notebook, and nobody is going to open a notebook just to ask a PDF a question. I wanted something you could actually open and use, and that wouldn't make stuff up when the answer wasn't in your files.

What it does
  • Upload documents into a desktop app and ask questions about them
  • Answers come only from your files, with the source file cited
  • Auto-summarize, extract key info, and flag problems in each file
  • Run several of those actions on every upload at once
How it works
Chunking
Text gets split into roughly 500-character chunks with 100 characters of overlap, so a sentence cut in half still shows up whole in one of them.
Embeddings
A small local model, all-MiniLM-L6-v2, turns each chunk into a vector. It's free and runs on your machine.
Retrieval
Chunks below a similarity cutoff get dropped, and the ones that pass pull in their neighbors for extra context.
Answering
Claude Haiku writes the answer from only those chunks and cites the files it used. If nothing relevant comes back, it says "I couldn't find that in your documents" instead of guessing.
API
Separate endpoints for upload, ask, summarize, extract, flag, listing documents, and deleting them.
How it came together
  • JuneWorking RAG pipeline from the command line
  • JuneTauri desktop app and FastAPI server
  • AugustUpload, delete, and grounded Q&A, then auto-summarize with tabs and an activity feed, then extract and flag actions
  • OctoberWired every enabled action into the upload flow
What I learned

Getting the desktop side and the Python side to talk to each other cleanly was harder than the actual AI part. That's been true for almost every project I've done. The glue is usually the hard part. I also learned to load heavy stuff like the embedding model once when the server starts, instead of on every request, which made it way faster.

SubleaseHer

Full-stack sublease marketplace for college students2026
Live site ↗Source code ↗
Why I built it

Finding a sublease in college is a mess. It's group chats, random Instagram posts, and spreadsheets, and you end up giving your number to strangers. I wanted one place where students could post a place, filter for what they actually need, and message each other without leaving the site.

What it does
  • Accounts with profiles: name, pronouns, university, bio, and photo
  • Create, edit, pause, or delete listings with photo galleries
  • Search by keyword, location, price range, beds, and move-in dates
  • Message other users about a specific listing, with unread counts
  • Save favorites and leave 1 to 5 star reviews
  • A dashboard with your stats, listings, messages, and profile
The data model

I designed the schema around five tables: users, listings, messages, favorites, and reviews. Messages are tied to a listing, so every conversation has context, and reviews are tied to a user so people build a track record.

What I learned

This was my first time owning an app from the database all the way to deployment. The biggest lesson was security. It's easy to build a page that works, but you have to make sure nobody can edit someone else's listing just by changing a URL, so every route that changes data checks who you are first. I also learned to keep the search and filter logic in one shared place, so the browse page and the API never disagree.

NBA Player DNA

Clustering 500+ players by how they actually play2026
Live dashboard ↗Source code ↗

Why I built it

Listed positions don't say much about how someone actually plays anymore. I wanted to see what you'd get if you let the stats group players instead, and then use that to answer a real question: who would actually fit on a given roster?

What it does
  • Player profiles with stat cards, a shot chart, and where they sit on the cluster map
  • Side-by-side player comparison
  • A team fit analyzer that ranks free agent targets
  • League overview and season-by-season trends
How it works
Who's included
Players with at least 20 games and 15 minutes a game, so small samples don't throw off the clusters.
The stats
Scoring and efficiency (points, true shooting, threes per 36 and 3P%), playmaking (assist rate, assist to turnover, turnover ratio, usage), rebounding (offensive and defensive rebound rates), defense (steals and blocks per 36, defensive rating), plus offensive rating and PIE.
The archetypes
  • Playmaking Star
  • Playmaking Guard
  • Scoring Wing
  • 3-and-D Wing
  • Two-Way Glue Guy
  • Versatile Big
  • Rim Anchor
  • Bench Perimeter Player
Team fit score
Weighted 55% on how well a player fills a team's missing archetypes, 25% on overall impact (PIE), and 10% each on scoring and assists.
What I learned

Naming the clusters was honestly harder than building them. The math groups players together, but it doesn't tell you what to call the group or whether it makes sense to someone who actually watches the games. I also learned how much the filters matter. Without the games and minutes cutoffs, a guy who played three minutes once can end up looking like a superstar.

EV Registration Analysis

Database design and SQL on 100K+ Washington EV records2025

I took a big flat dataset of Washington State EV registrations and designed a proper relational database for it. That meant splitting it into separate tables, normalizing to third normal form so nothing is stored twice, and drawing an ER diagram before writing any SQL.

Then I wrote queries to look at adoption by county and vehicle type. The top 3 manufacturers make up 78% of all registrations, which is way more concentrated than I expected. I also pulled out the counties with the highest adoption, which is the kind of thing that matters for deciding where chargers should go.

What I learned

Designing the schema first saved me later. Once the tables were clean, the analysis queries were a lot shorter and easier to trust.

  • SQL
  • Database design
  • 3NF
  • ER diagrams
