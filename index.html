Research

INST408G · Fall 2026

Extracting claims and evidence from research papers: frontier vs. local models vs. human review

Abbilash Shivaparan, Ayomikun Ogunmolasuyi · Advised by Prof. Joel Chan · University of Maryland
Summary

Living reviews only work if breaking new papers into claims and evidence is cheap. We're testing whether AI can do that well enough to trust. Early review suggests a lot of what the AI writes still needs a person to fix it, and some of those fixes change what a result actually means.

The problem

Synthesis is when you pull together everything a field knows about one question, and it takes forever. One review we looked at screened almost 3,700 references just to end up with 28 studies. On top of that, its search stopped in 2005, so nothing newer is in it. "Living" reviews fix that by updating every time a new study comes out, but that only works if updating is cheap.

The expensive part is breaking each new paper down into its claims and the evidence behind them. Joel's lab stores research that way in something called a discourse graph, where every claim and every piece of evidence is its own node and they all link to each other. If AI could do that breakdown, living reviews would actually be doable. So our question is pretty simple: is AI good enough at it that we can trust it?

Here's what that actually looks like. This is one small corner of the lab's graph, pulled straight from the vault: one research question, the claim that answers it, the evidence behind that claim, and two patterns that show up across papers.

Data and method

27papers on using AI to evaluate research
205nodes already drafted by a frontier model
0of those reviewed by a person when we started
3sources compared: frontier model, local model, human review

Early findings

A reviewer in the lab goes through the AI's nodes and labels each one as Correct, Edit, or Delete, with a short note about why. So far, most of the nodes that have been reviewed needed at least some editing. Some research questions even had to be split into two.

To be fair, "Edit" doesn't mean the AI was totally wrong, and a lot of the fixes are small. So we grouped the reviewer's notes by what was actually wrong, and they fell into two kinds of problems.

The most common issue was missing context, like leaving out which model a study tested or how many papers were in it. The ones that worry me more are the reframes, where the way the AI wrote a node changed what the result actually meant. In one case, fixing it flipped a result from looking bad to looking really good. That's exactly the kind of mistake you can't let slide into a review people rely on.

How the plan changed

Honestly, our project looks pretty different from what we pitched in our first lightning talk. Once we got into the real data, we found out half the study basically already existed. The AI's output was sitting right there, and what was missing was the human side to compare it against.

This version is more realistic for one semester. It's also closer to how a living review would really work, where a person checks what the AI drafts instead of doing everything by hand.

My role

I'm leading the local model and the scoring. That means setting up an open model, running it on the same 27 papers, and building the scoring that compares it and the frontier model against the human reviews. Ayomikun leads the node review and our labeling guide. We both review the same 10 nodes first so we know our labels actually hold up before we split the rest.

Why I built it

Listed positions don't say much about how someone actually plays anymore. I wanted to see what you'd get if you let the stats group players instead, and use that to answer a real question: who would actually fit on a given roster?

What it does
  • A pipeline that pulls traditional and advanced stats into SQLite
  • PCA to cut the stats down, then K-Means into 8 archetypes

What I learned

Naming the clusters was honestly harder than building them. The math groups players together, but it doesn't tell you what to call the group or whether it makes sense to someone who actually watches the games.

Young Voter Outreach

Research pitch to New Carrollton's city council2025

New Carrollton has talked about letting 16 and 17 year olds vote in local elections. If that happens, the city suddenly has a group of voters who probably don't follow city channels at all. Our pitch was simple: don't assume teens look for information the same way adults do. Find out first.

We designed an online survey about where teens would actually look, what confuses them, and which formats would help, plus one open question so they could tell us what we missed. We also used 2010 to 2020 Census data to show the city is growing and getting more diverse, which is why we recommended going through schools, community networks, and more than one language instead of just the city website.

  • Survey design
